# PT Learning Lab — Squat-led Prototype Learning Plan

Status: approved learning contract for Phase 1 implementation

Date: 5 August 2026
Depends on: `PT_LEARNING_LAB_DECISIONS.md` and `PT_LEARNING_LAB_PRODUCT_BLUEPRINT.md`

## 1. Purpose of this plan

This document defines the learning outcomes and interactions for the first polished prototype: **Anatomical language and movement at a joint**, taught through a recurring squat example.

It turns the five-lesson sequence in the product blueprint into a wireframe-ready learning flow. It does not specify production technology, final artwork or copied course content.

## 2. Prototype learning promise

By the end of the prototype, a learner should be able to look at a simple exercise image or movement sequence and describe it using the qualification vocabulary of:

- anatomical position and relative positional terms;
- the three planes of motion and their corresponding axes;
- flexion, extension and the principal joint actions visible in a squat;
- the predominant plane and joint actions in a small set of common exercises.

The prototype teaches a method, not a list to memorise:

1. Establish the reference position and viewpoint.
2. Identify the body part or joint being discussed.
3. Observe the direction of movement or change in joint angle.
4. Name the joint action.
5. Identify the predominant plane and corresponding axis.

## 3. Scope boundaries

### Included in the five lessons

- Anatomical position.
- Anterior/posterior, medial/lateral, superior/inferior, proximal/distal and superficial/deep.
- Sagittal, frontal and transverse planes.
- Medio-lateral, anterior-posterior and longitudinal axes.
- Flexion and extension at the hip and knee.
- Dorsiflexion and the return towards neutral at the ankle during a squat.
- Recognition of abduction/adduction and rotation through carefully chosen secondary movements.
- Applying the vocabulary to static images and short movement sequences.

### Deferred to later curriculum content

- Detailed skeletal landmarks, joint classifications and muscle anatomy.
- Origins, insertions, muscle roles and contraction types.
- Biomechanical analysis, force, moment arms and movement-efficiency claims.
- Technique assessment, injury diagnosis or prescriptive squat correction.
- Exhaustive mastery of every joint action listed in the course reference table.

The prototype may name a visible action without judging whether a person’s movement is “good”, “bad”, safe or unsafe.

## 5. Shared lesson rhythm

Each lesson should take approximately 5–10 minutes and use the same seven-part rhythm:

1. **Hook** — one practical visual question.
2. **Outcome** — one concise “By the end, you can…” statement.
3. **Explain** — no more than three short concept blocks.
4. **Explore** — one primary interactive visual.
5. **Apply** — one squat-led or PT-relevant use of the concept.
6. **Check** — two or three retrieval questions with immediate explanatory feedback.
7. **Close** — summary, confidence prompt and a transparent next recommendation.

Each interaction must also have:

- full keyboard operation;
- a visible focus state;
- a non-drag alternative where dragging is offered;
- instructions that do not depend on colour;
- a structured-text equivalent for diagrams;
- answer feedback announced to assistive technology;
- a restart control that does not erase the learner’s wider progress.

## 6. Lesson specifications

### Lesson 1 — Anatomical position and directional terms

**Learner outcome**

By the end, you can use anatomical position as a shared reference and accurately compare the location of two body structures.

**Supporting outcomes**

The learner can:

- recognise the essential features of anatomical position;
- explain why left and right belong to the person being described, not the viewer;
- correctly apply the five paired terms in scope;
- distinguish a positional term from a movement description.

**Hook**

Show front and back views of an original standing figure and ask: “Whose left side are we describing?” The answer establishes the subject-relative viewpoint before terminology is introduced.

**Explain**

- Anatomical position is the agreed reference for describing the body.
- Directional terms compare one structure with another; they are not loose substitutes for everyday directions.
- The comparison must name or make clear both structures.

**Primary interaction — Reference-position lab**

The learner toggles between front and back views, then selects the features that belong to anatomical position. Incorrect selections explain the misconception, especially viewer-left versus subject-left.

**Apply interaction — Complete the comparison**

The learner completes five short statements using labelled body landmarks, for example choosing the relationship between the hip and knee or the patella and structures behind it. A pair-by-pair reveal shows the relevant reference line.

The squat appears only after the reference position has been introduced and checked: the learner compares the locations of structures on the standing figure before viewing the same figure in the starting squat stance. The teaching point is that positional language remains anchored to the body, while the pose changes.

**Check formats**

- Two-option comparison: medial or lateral; proximal or distal.
- Error spotting: a coach uses the viewer’s left instead of the client’s left.
- One “not a movement term” discrimination item.

**Misconceptions to target**

- Left/right changes with the viewer’s position.
- Proximal/distal means simply higher/lower.
- Anterior always means the direction a person is moving.
- Superficial/deep means visually obvious/hidden in an illustration.

**Glossary seeded**

Anatomical position, midline, anterior, posterior, medial, lateral, superior, inferior, proximal, distal, superficial and deep.

### Lesson 2 — Planes and axes

**Learner outcome**

By the end, you can match each anatomical plane to its axis and identify the predominant plane of a simple movement.

**Supporting outcomes**

The learner can:

- describe how each plane divides the body;
- pair sagittal with the medio-lateral axis, frontal with the anterior-posterior axis and transverse with the longitudinal axis;
- distinguish the plane through which movement occurs from the axis around which it occurs;
- identify a squat as predominantly sagittal-plane movement.

**Hook**

Show a three-stage squat sequence and ask the learner to place one of three translucent plane cards through the figure. The initial answer is treated as a prediction, not a scored result.

**Explain**

Use a single original figure with one plane visible at a time. Each state shows:

- the portions into which the plane divides the body;
- a plainly drawn axis line at right angles to the plane;
- one representative movement.

**Primary interaction — Plane-and-axis explorer**

Selecting a plane rotates or changes the diagram to show its corresponding axis. The structured-text view lists the same relationship. The learner then pairs all three plane and axis cards.

**Apply interaction — Predominant plane sorter**

Sort three movements:

- bodyweight squat — sagittal;
- lateral raise or side step — frontal;
- standing torso rotation — transverse.

These secondary movements are included because the squat cannot clearly demonstrate all three planes. Copy should use “predominantly” and acknowledge that real movement may include smaller components in other planes.

**Check formats**

- Match a plane to its axis.
- Identify the plane represented by a body division.
- Error spotting: “The axis is the direction the body part travels.”

**Misconceptions to target**

- A plane and its axis are the same thing.
- Sagittal always means forward movement rather than a plane through the body.
- An exercise belongs absolutely to one plane.

**Glossary seeded**

Plane of motion, axis, sagittal plane, frontal plane, transverse plane, medio-lateral axis, anterior-posterior axis, longitudinal axis and predominant movement.

### Lesson 3 — Joint actions in a squat

**Learner outcome**

By the end, you can identify the principal hip, knee and ankle actions during the lowering and rising phases of a bodyweight squat.

**Supporting outcomes**

The learner can:

- infer flexion or extension from a decreasing or increasing joint angle;
- identify hip and knee flexion during descent;
- identify hip and knee extension during ascent;
- identify ankle dorsiflexion during descent and movement back towards neutral during ascent;
- state which joint and movement phase an answer describes.

**Hook**

Show the standing and bottom positions with one joint marker highlighted. Ask: “At which joint is the angle decreasing?”

**Explain**

Use an original three-stage side-view squat: start, lower position and return. Joint arcs appear one at a time, never all at once. The copy separates joint action from muscle action and from technique judgement.

**Primary interaction — Scrubbable squat sequence**

The learner moves through the three stages using a slider, previous/next buttons or arrow keys. Selecting hip, knee or ankle reveals the angle change and asks the learner to name the action. A structured sequence table provides the equivalent information without motion.

**Apply interaction — Build the phase description**

For descent and ascent, the learner assembles a sentence from three parts:

`phase` + `joint` + `action`

For example: “During the descent, the knee moves into flexion.” The ankle ascent wording should be “moves from dorsiflexion back towards neutral” in the main teaching copy; the deeper reference can relate this to a plantarflexion action without implying a calf raise.

**Check formats**

- Choose the action at a highlighted joint and phase.
- Order three frames from standing to the bottom position.
- Error spotting: an answer names a muscle when the question asks for a joint action.

**Misconceptions to target**

- Flexion always means a limb travels forward.
- Every joint performs the same action during one phase.
- The lowering phase is itself called flexion without naming a joint.
- Joint action and muscle contraction type are interchangeable.

**Glossary seeded**

Joint action, flexion, extension, dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, hip joint, knee joint and ankle joint.

### Lesson 4 — Recognising actions in common exercises

**Learner outcome**

By the end, you can use joint, phase and viewpoint clues to identify actions in unfamiliar exercise images.

**Supporting outcomes**

The learner can:

- follow the five-step analysis method rather than guess from the exercise name;
- recognise flexion/extension, abduction/adduction and rotation in clear examples;
- identify the predominant plane for each example;
- explain an answer in one short evidence-based sentence.

**Hook**

Show two visually different exercises that both involve flexion and ask what they have in common at the highlighted joint.

**Explain**

Introduce the analysis method from section 2 as a reusable checklist. Model it once with the squat, including the precise joint and phase.

**Primary interaction — Movement detective**

The learner analyses four original two- or three-frame movement cards:

- squat — hip/knee flexion and extension, predominantly sagittal;
- elbow curl — elbow flexion and extension, predominantly sagittal;
- lateral raise — shoulder abduction and adduction, predominantly frontal;
- controlled standing torso rotation — spinal/trunk rotation, predominantly transverse.

Each card asks for joint or region, phase/direction, action and predominant plane. Later cards remove more prompts.

**Apply interaction — Diagnose the explanation**

The learner chooses which of three short coaching explanations is precise enough. Feedback rewards naming the joint, phase and observable change, not longer wording.

**Check formats**

- Label a highlighted action.
- Match an action to a plane in a specific example.
- Select the best explanation and identify what is missing from weaker ones.

**Misconceptions to target**

- The exercise name alone determines the joint action.
- Up always means extension and down always means flexion.
- Abduction/adduction can be identified without reference to the body’s midline.
- One visible exercise position is enough to infer movement direction.

**Glossary seeded**

Abduction, adduction, rotation, movement phase and range of motion.

### Lesson 5 — Mixed movement challenge

**Learner outcome**

By the end, you can combine anatomical position, directional language, planes, axes and joint actions to analyse a short movement sequence.

**Supporting outcomes**

The learner can:

- retrieve concepts without following the lesson order;
- apply the analysis method to both familiar and fresh examples;
- explain at least one answer rather than only select a label;
- review mistakes by misconception and choose what to revisit next.

**Hook**

Present the challenge as a lab case: “Describe what changes from standing to the bottom of this squat so another trainer could understand you precisely.”

**Primary interaction — Six-step movement case**

Use six varied tasks, one per screen on mobile and in a calm sequence on laptop:

1. Set the subject-relative viewpoint.
2. Complete a directional comparison.
3. Select the squat’s predominant plane.
4. Match that plane to its axis.
5. Label hip and knee actions in one phase.
6. Spot and repair an imprecise movement description.

At least two items should use a fresh secondary movement so success is not only recognition of the repeated squat artwork.

**Feedback and close**

Feedback remains immediate, but the final summary groups performance by learning outcome rather than showing only a percentage. It should say, for example:

- “Strong in this challenge: directional comparisons.”
- “Check again soon: matching planes and axes.”
- “Why this is recommended: you reversed the plane-axis pair twice.”

The learner can accept the recommended revision item, choose another lesson or continue. One challenge cannot produce the state **Secure** by itself; it contributes evidence towards security over time and across question forms.

**Challenge variations required before pilot**

- Two squat visual variants with different body proportions and clothing.
- At least one alternate viewpoint that does not make left/right ambiguous.
- One frontal-plane and one transverse-plane transfer example.
- Distractors tied to named misconceptions, not random wrong terms.

## 7. Feedback model

Every checked response has four possible feedback components:

1. **Result** — correct, partly correct or needs another look.
2. **Reason** — the visual or conceptual evidence for the result.
3. **Misconception** — only when a selected answer reveals a known confusion.
4. **Next action** — retry, inspect the visual, open a glossary term or add a revision item.

Correct feedback should still teach. Incorrect feedback should not reveal the answer before explaining the relevant clue. Language should be direct and adult; avoid celebratory noise, punishment language or false claims of mastery.

## 8. Prototype progress and revision rules

- Opening a lesson sets it to **In progress**.
- Completing its close step sets it to **Learned**, regardless of score.
- Answering at least one meaningful check records **Practised** evidence for the relevant outcome.
- A wrong or partly correct answer creates or strengthens a revision reason tied to the outcome and misconception.
- A confidence response changes recommendation priority only; it never overrides correctness.
- **Secure** requires correct retrieval on more than one occasion and in more than one question form. The vertical slice must demonstrate this rule, even if road-testing compresses the elapsed time.
- The learner can dismiss, postpone or manually choose revision, and the interface explains the consequence without pressure.

The prototype needs revision reasons for at least:

- subject-left versus viewer-left;
- proximal/distal confusion;
- plane/axis reversal;
- flexion/extension reversal;
- failure to name the joint or phase;
- abduction/adduction reversal;
- overgeneralising one plane to all movement.

These are content requirements for later illustration planning, not assets to create now:

1. Neutral figure in anatomical position: front, back and side views.
2. Directional-term overlays with landmark comparisons.
3. Plane-and-axis explorer with three accessible states.
4. Three-stage bodyweight squat: standing, descent/bottom and return.
5. Joint-focused squat overlays for hip, knee and ankle.
6. Elbow curl sequence.
7. Lateral raise sequence.
8. Standing torso-rotation sequence.

Artwork must be inclusive, anatomically checked, editable, legible at phone size and accompanied by structured text. It must not trace or stylistically reproduce the source-book diagrams.

## 12. Acceptance checks for the learning design

Before moving from wireframes to implementation, owner review should confirm that:

- each lesson has one assessable learner outcome and no hidden expansion of scope;
- the five lessons form a coherent progression but remain individually revisit-able;
- every primary interaction teaches something that static prose alone would teach less clearly;
- all answers can be completed without dragging, colour perception or animation;
- the squat remains the recurring anchor without being forced into frontal or transverse examples;
- the copy consistently names the joint and phase when describing an action;
- the course mapping discrepancy for joint actions is resolved or transparently retained as pending;
- no source visual, protected question or distinctive course wording has entered the prototype;
- a wrong answer produces an understandable and overridable revision recommendation;
- one successful attempt is never presented as durable security.
